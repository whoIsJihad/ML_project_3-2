# Sampling

**Sampling** is the process of converting a continuous-time signal into a discrete-time signal. This is achieved by taking measurements, or samples, of the signal at discrete intervals of time.

## Principle

Let a continuous-time signal be denoted by $m(t)$. The sampling process produces a discrete-time signal, $m[n]$, by capturing the value of $m(t)$ at time instants $t = nT_s$, where $T_s$ is the **sampling period**.

$m[n] = m(nT_s)$

The **sampling rate** or **sampling frequency**, denoted by $f_s$, is the reciprocal of the sampling period:

$f_s = \frac{1}{T_s}$

It represents the number of samples taken per second.

## The Sampling Theorem

The [[Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem]] establishes the fundamental condition required to perfectly reconstruct a continuous-time signal from its samples. It states that a bandlimited signal, with a maximum frequency component of $f_{max}$, can be fully recovered from its samples if the sampling rate $f_s$ is greater than twice the maximum frequency.

$f_s > 2f_{max}$

The minimum sampling rate required for perfect reconstruction, $2f_{max}$, is known as the **Nyquist rate**.

## Mathematical Representation

The process of sampling can be modeled as the multiplication of the continuous-time signal $m(t)$ with an impulse train $\delta_{T_s}(t)$, which is a series of [[Unit Impulse Function|Dirac delta functions]] spaced $T_s$ apart.

$\delta_{T_s}(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t - nT_s)$

The sampled signal, $m_s(t)$, is then:

$m_s(t) = m(t) \cdot \delta_{T_s}(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} m(nT_s) \delta(t - nT_s)$
