# Signal-to-Noise Ratio in PCM

The **Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR or S/N)** is a critical metric used to quantify the performance of a communication system. In the context of Pulse Code Modulation (PCM), it measures the ratio of the desired signal power to the unwanted [[2.3 Quantization Noise|quantization noise]] power.

## Definition

The SNR is formally defined as:

$SNR = \frac{\text{Average Signal Power}}{\text{Average Noise Power}} = \frac{P_S}{P_N}$

In PCM, the dominant source of noise is quantization noise, so we use the quantization noise power, $N_q$. The signal power, $P_S$, is the average power of the message signal $m(t)$, which is denoted as $\overline{m^2(t)}$.

$SNR_{PCM} = \frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{N_q}$

## Derivation for Uniform Quantization

From the analysis of quantization noise, we have the expression for the average noise power:

$N_q = \frac{m_p^2}{3L^2}$

Where:
-   $m_p$ is the peak amplitude of the signal.
-   $L$ is the number of quantization levels.

Substituting this into the SNR equation gives:

$SNR_{PCM} = \frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2 / (3L^2)} = 3L^2 \frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2}$

This is the general formula for the output SNR of a uniform PCM system.

## Relationship with Number of Bits (n)

The number of quantization levels, $L$, is related to the number of bits per sample, $n$, by $L = 2^n$. Substituting this into the SNR formula:

$SNR_{PCM} = 3(2^n)^2 \frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2} = 3 \cdot 2^{2n} \frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2}$

This equation shows that the SNR increases exponentially with the number of bits used per sample.

## SNR in Decibels (dB)

It is common to express SNR in decibels (dB):

$SNR_{dB} = 10 \log_{10}(SNR)$

$SNR_{dB} = 10 \log_{10}\left(3 \cdot 2^{2n} \frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2}\right)$

$SNR_{dB} = 10 \log_{10}(3) + 10 \log_{10}(2^{2n}) + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2}\right)$

Using the logarithm property $\log(x^y) = y \log(x)$ and approximating values:
-   $10 \log_{10}(3) \approx 4.77$ dB
-   $10 \log_{10}(2^{2n}) = 20n \log_{10}(2) \approx 20n \times 0.301 \approx 6.02n$ dB

So, the expression becomes:

$SNR_{dB} \approx 4.77 + 6.02n + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2}\right)$

This formula highlights a very important relationship known as the [[2.7 The 6-dB Rule|6-dB Rule]]. The term $10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2}\right)$ depends on the characteristics of the input signal, specifically its crest factor.

For a normalized sinusoidal signal, $m(t) = m_p \cos(\omega t)$, the average power is $\overline{m^2(t)} = \frac{m_p^2}{2}$. The SNR formula simplifies to:

$SNR_{PCM, sinusoid} = 3L^2 \frac{m_p^2/2}{m_p^2} = 1.5 L^2 = 1.5 \cdot 2^{2n}$

In dB:
$SNR_{dB} = 10 \log_{10}(1.5) + 10 \log_{10}(2^{2n}) \approx 1.76 + 6.02n$

This shows that for a full-scale sinusoidal input, the SNR is directly determined by the number of bits, $n$.
