# Companding

**Companding** is a portmanteau of **compressing** and **expanding**. It is the practical technique used to implement [[2.5 Non-Uniform Quantization (Hard)|non-uniform quantization]] in digital communication systems, particularly in the [[2.1 PCM System Overview|PCM]] systems used for digital telephony.

## The Companding Process

The process involves two main steps: compression at the transmitter and expansion at the receiver.

### 1. Compression (at the Transmitter)

Before the signal is quantized, it is passed through a **compressor**. A compressor is a non-linear amplifier that has a carefully designed input-output characteristic curve.

-   **High Gain for Low Amplitudes:** The compressor provides high amplification for weak (low-amplitude) input signals.
-   **Low Gain for High Amplitudes:** It provides low (or unity) amplification for strong (high-amplitude) input signals.

The effect of compression is to "squash" the dynamic range of the signal. The variations in the amplitudes of the compressed signal are smaller than in the original signal. Specifically, the quiet parts of the signal are made louder, bringing them up from the noise floor, while the loud parts are kept at a similar level.

After compression, the signal is fed into a simple [[2.2 Uniform Quantization|uniform quantizer]]. Because the low-amplitude signals have been boosted, they now span a larger number of quantization levels than they would have without compression. This effectively creates a finer resolution for quiet sounds.

### 2. Expansion (at the Receiver)

After the digital signal is received and converted back into quantized amplitude levels by the decoder, it must be restored to its original dynamic range. This is done by an **expander**.

The expander has an input-output characteristic that is the exact inverse of the compressor's characteristic.

-   **Low Gain for Low Amplitudes:** It attenuates the weak signals to restore them to their original quiet levels.
-   **High Gain for High Amplitudes:** It amplifies the strong signals to restore them to their original loud levels.

This expansion process undoes the compression, resulting in a signal that has the same dynamic characteristics as the original analog signal, but with the benefits of improved SNR for the quiet passages.

## 3. Simplified Example: How a Signal is Processed

Let's consider a simplified companding process with a 5-level uniform quantizer, covering the range [0, 1]. The quantization levels are 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0. We'll use a simple compression function $y = \sqrt{x}$ and its inverse expansion function $x = y^2$ (assuming positive signals for simplicity).

Consider four analog input values: 0.05, 0.10, 0.40, and 0.85.

| Analog Input (x) | Compressed Signal (y = $\sqrt{x}$) | Uniformly Quantized (y_q) | Expanded Signal (x_r = y_q$^2$) | Original Quantization Error (without companding) | Companded Quantization Error |
| :--------------- | :--------------------------------- | :------------------------ | :------------------------------ | :----------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------- |
| 0.05             | 0.223                            | 0.25                      | 0.0625                          | 0.05 - 0 = 0.05                                  | 0.05 - 0.0625 = -0.0125      |
| 0.10             | 0.316                            | 0.25                      | 0.0625                          | 0.10 - 0 = 0.10                                  | 0.10 - 0.0625 = 0.0375       |
| 0.40             | 0.632                            | 0.75                      | 0.5625                          | 0.40 - 0.5 = -0.10                               | 0.40 - 0.5625 = -0.1625      |
| 0.85             | 0.922                            | 1.0                       | 1.0                             | 0.85 - 1.0 = -0.15                               | 0.85 - 1.0 = -0.15           |

**Analysis:**

*   **Without Companding (direct uniform quantization):** Small signals like 0.05 and 0.10 would both be quantized to 0, resulting in significant errors (0.05 and 0.10 respectively).
*   **With Companding:**
    *   The compressor effectively "stretches" the lower amplitude values across more quantization levels. For instance, 0.05 and 0.10, when compressed, fall into different segments of the uniform quantizer, allowing them to be distinguished.
    *   After expansion, even though the signals are slightly distorted, the *relative* error for the smaller signals is often reduced compared to direct uniform quantization, leading to a better perceived quality. The quantization noise is redistributed such that it is more evenly spread across the dynamic range of the original signal.

This example demonstrates how companding effectively allocates more quantization levels to smaller (more frequent) signal amplitudes, thereby improving their representation accuracy and reducing perceived quantization noise.

## 4. Purpose and Advantage

The primary purpose of companding is to achieve a nearly constant [[2.4 Signal-to-Noise Ratio in PCM|Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)]] over a wide range of input signal power levels.

-   **Without Companding (Uniform Quantization):** SNR is high for strong signals but very low for weak signals.
-   **With Companding:** Weak signals are amplified before quantization, making them much stronger relative to the fixed [[2.3 Quantization Noise|quantization noise]] floor. While the expansion process at the receiver scales the signal back down, it also scales down the quantization noise associated with it. The result is a significant improvement in SNR for weak signals. Strong signals, which already have a good SNR, are largely unaffected.

This technique allows a PCM system with a limited number of bits (e.g., 8 bits) to perform as well as a uniform quantization system with more bits (e.g., 11 or 12 bits), saving a significant amount of bandwidth. The two standard companding algorithms, **[[2.5 Non-Uniform Quantization (Hard)|μ-Law and A-Law]]**, are foundational to the modern public switched telephone network (PSTN).

## 5. Visualizing Companding Characteristics (μ-Law and A-Law)

The effectiveness of companding relies on specific non-linear input-output relationships provided by compressor functions. The two most widely used companding standards are μ-Law (predominantly in North America and Japan) and A-Law (used in Europe and most of the rest of the world).

While the mathematical formulas for these laws are detailed in [[2.5 Non-Uniform Quantization (Hard)|Non-Uniform Quantization]], their functional characteristics can be summarized as follows:

*   **μ-Law:**
    *   Applies a logarithmic compression across the entire dynamic range.
    *   Provides greater compression for higher amplitude signals and less compression for lower amplitude signals, effectively boosting the relative strength of quiet sounds.
    *   The `mu` parameter (typically 255) defines the degree of compression.

*   **A-Law:**
    *   Employs a piecewise linear (linear segment near zero) and logarithmic (for higher amplitudes) compression characteristic.
    *   The linear segment for very small signals helps to reduce distortion at extremely low amplitudes compared to a purely logarithmic curve.
    *   The `A` parameter (typically 87.6) defines the compression curve.

Both μ-Law and A-Law compressors aim to transform the original analog signal's amplitude distribution so that it better matches the uniform step sizes of a subsequent quantizer. This ensures that the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) after quantization is more consistent across a wider range of input signal power levels, leading to a more pleasant listening experience, especially for speech signals.

## 6. Conceptual Simulation Workflow

To fully grasp the impact of companding, one can conceptualize a simulation process that demonstrates its effects on a signal. Such a simulation would typically involve the following steps:

1.  **Signal Generation:** Generate a representative analog signal. For audio applications, this would ideally be a segment of speech or music, characterized by varying amplitudes. For simpler demonstrations, a sine wave with varying amplitude or a combination of sine waves could be used.

2.  **Scenario Setup:**
    *   **Scenario A (Without Companding):** The generated analog signal is directly fed into a uniform quantizer.
    *   **Scenario B (With Companding):** The generated analog signal is first passed through a compressor (e.g., μ-Law or A-Law), and then the compressed signal is fed into the same uniform quantizer as in Scenario A.

3.  **Quantization:** In both scenarios, the signals (original in A, compressed in B) are uniformly quantized using a defined number of bits (e.g., 8 bits).

4.  **Digital Transmission (Conceptual):** The quantized digital values would typically be transmitted. For a simulation focusing on processing, this step is implicit; we proceed directly to decoding.

5.  **Decoding and Expansion:**
    *   **Scenario A:** The uniformly quantized signal is directly decoded back into analog form.
    *   **Scenario B:** The uniformly quantized signal (from the compressed input) is first decoded, and then passed through an expander (the inverse of the compressor used) to restore its original dynamic range.

6.  **Analysis and Comparison:**
    *   **Waveform Visualization:** Plot the original analog signal, the uniformly quantized signal (with and without companding), and the reconstructed analog signal (with and without companding). This visually highlights the differences in waveform distortion.
    *   **Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) Calculation:** Calculate the SNR for the reconstructed signals in both scenarios. Crucially, compute SNR across different amplitude ranges of the input signal. A companded system should show a more consistent and improved SNR for lower amplitude signals compared to a non-companded system.
    *   **Perceptual Evaluation:** (If audio signal) Play back the reconstructed audio from both scenarios. The companded version should sound clearer, especially during quieter passages, with less noticeable quantization noise.

By following this workflow, the tangible benefits of companding in maintaining a consistent SNR and improving perceived signal quality, particularly for signals with wide dynamic ranges like speech, become evident.
