# Non-Uniform Quantization

**Non-uniform quantization** is a [[1.2 Quantization|quantization]] technique where the step size is not constant across the entire dynamic range of the signal. The quantization levels are spaced farther apart for larger signal amplitudes and closer together for smaller signal amplitudes.

## Motivation

In [[2.2 Uniform Quantization|uniform quantization]], the [[2.3 Quantization Noise|quantization noise]] power is constant, regardless of the signal's amplitude. This results in a low [[2.4 Signal-to-Noise Ratio in PCM|Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)]] for low-amplitude signals, which can be a significant problem for signals like speech.

In a typical speech signal, small amplitudes (corresponding to quiet sounds and pauses) occur much more frequently than large amplitudes (loud sounds). Uniform quantization allocates an equal number of bits to represent both, which is inefficient. The perceived quality of speech is heavily dependent on the fidelity of the low-volume portions.

Non-uniform quantization addresses this by providing more resolution (and thus less noise) for the more probable, smaller-amplitude signals, at the expense of less resolution for the less frequent, larger-amplitude signals. The goal is to make the SNR roughly constant across the entire range of signal power levels.

## Principle

Instead of having a fixed step size $\Delta V$, a non-uniform quantizer uses a variable step size. The relationship between the input signal level and the quantized output level is non-linear.

This can be achieved in two primary ways:

1.  **Direct Implementation:** Design a quantizer with non-uniformly spaced decision thresholds and reconstruction levels. This is complex to implement directly.
2.  **[[2.6 Companding|Companding]] (Compression + Expanding):** This is the universally adopted practical method. The process involves:
    *   **Compression:** At the transmitter, the analog signal is passed through a non-linear compressor circuit before being quantized. The compressor amplifies the low-amplitude parts of the signal more than the high-amplitude parts.
    *   **Uniform Quantization:** The compressed signal, which now has a more uniform amplitude distribution, is fed into a standard [[2.2 Uniform Quantization|uniform quantizer]].
    *   **Expansion:** At the receiver, the decoded signal is passed through an expander circuit, which has the inverse characteristic of the compressor. This restores the original signal dynamics.

The combination of a compressor and an expander is called a **compander**.

## Common Companding Laws

Two international standards for companding are widely used in digital telephony:

1.  **μ-Law (or u-Law):** Used primarily in North America and Japan. The compression characteristic is logarithmic and is defined by the formula:
    $F(x) = \text{sgn}(x) \frac{\ln(1 + \mu|x|)}{\ln(1 + \mu)}$
    Where $x$ is the normalized input signal ($|x| \le 1$) and $\mu$ is a parameter that defines the amount of compression (a typical value for telephony is $\mu=255$).

2.  **A-Law:** Used in Europe and most of the rest of the world. Its characteristic is also logarithmic but is piecewise linear around the origin to avoid issues with zero-level signals. The formula is:
    $F(x) = \text{sgn}(x) \begin{cases} \frac{A|x|}{1 + \ln(A)} & \text{for } |x| < \frac{1}{A} \\ \frac{1 + \ln(A|x|)}{1 + \ln(A)} & \text{for } \frac{1}{A} \le |x| \le 1 \end{cases}$
    A typical value for the parameter $A$ is 87.6.

Both laws significantly improve the SNR for low-level signals compared to uniform quantization, making them essential for high-quality digital voice communication.
