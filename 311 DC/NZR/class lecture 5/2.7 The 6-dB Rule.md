# The 6-dB Rule

The **6-dB Rule** is a well-known rule of thumb in [[2.1 PCM System Overview|Pulse Code Modulation (PCM)]] that quantifies the relationship between the number of bits used for quantization and the resulting [[2.4 Signal-to-Noise Ratio in PCM|Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)]]. The rule states that for each additional bit used in the quantization process, the SNR increases by approximately 6 decibels (dB).

## Derivation

The rule originates from the formula for the SNR of a PCM system, expressed in decibels. The general formula for SNR in dB is:

$SNR_{dB} \approx 4.77 + 6.02n + 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2}\right)$

Where:
-   $n$ is the number of bits per sample.
-   $\overline{m^2(t)}$ is the average power of the signal.
-   $m_p$ is the peak amplitude of the signal.

Let's analyze the components of this formula. The terms $4.77$ and $10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2}\right)$ are constant for a given type of signal. The only term that changes as we vary the number of quantization bits is the **6.02n** term.

This term comes directly from the relationship between the number of bits, the number of quantization levels ($L=2^n$), and the resulting [[2.3 Quantization Noise|quantization noise power]].

The non-decibel SNR formula is:
$SNR = 3L^2 \frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2} = 3(2^n)^2 \frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2} = 3 \cdot 2^{2n} \frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2}$

Let's consider the ratio of the SNR for $(n+1)$ bits to the SNR for $n$ bits.

$SNR_{n+1} = 3 \cdot 2^{2(n+1)} \frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2}$
$SNR_{n} = 3 \cdot 2^{2n} \frac{\overline{m^2(t)}}{m_p^2}$

$\frac{SNR_{n+1}}{SNR_{n}} = \frac{3 \cdot 2^{2n+2} \cdot (\dots)}{3 \cdot 2^{2n} \cdot (\dots)} = \frac{2^{2n} \cdot 2^2}{2^{2n}} = 4$

This shows that adding one bit quadruples the linear SNR value.

To see the effect in decibels:
Increase in dB = $10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{SNR_{n+1}}{SNR_{n}}\right) = 10 \log_{10}(4)$

Using the logarithm property $\log(2^2) = 2 \log(2)$:
Increase in dB = $10 \cdot 2 \log_{10}(2) = 20 \log_{10}(2) \approx 20 \times 0.30103 = 6.0206$ dB.

This is where the "6-dB Rule" comes from.

## Practical Implications

The 6-dB rule is a powerful and simple guideline for system design.

-   **Bandwidth vs. Quality Trade-off:** The data rate of a PCM signal is $R = f_s \times n$. Increasing the number of bits, $n$, improves the SNR (quality) but also linearly increases the data rate, and thus the required transmission bandwidth. The 6-dB rule allows a designer to quantify this trade-off precisely. For example, if a 12-dB improvement in audio quality is desired, the designer knows that this will require 2 additional bits per sample, which will increase the data rate by $f_s \times 2$.

-   **Audio Engineering:** In digital audio, the standard for CD quality is 16 bits. This provides a theoretical maximum SNR of approximately $1.76 + 6.02 \times 16 \approx 98$ dB. Professional audio systems often use 24-bit quantization, which provides a much larger dynamic range and a theoretical SNR of about 146 dB, allowing for more headroom and lower noise floors during recording and mixing. Each extra bit provides another 6 dB of dynamic range to work with.
