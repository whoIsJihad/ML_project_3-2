## Lesson 1: The Two Types of Observation

**Instructor:** Let me back up and teach you the theory first, then we'll apply it.

### **Non-Participant Observation** 👀
- You sit in the corner with a notebook
- You DON'T interact with anyone
- You're invisible, like a security camera
- **Pro:** People act naturally, you stay objective
- **Con:** You might misunderstand WHY they do things

### **Participant Observation** 🤝
- You actually BECOME a library user yourself
- You experience the space, talk to people, join their activities
- **Pro:** You understand their feelings, frustrations, motivations
- **Con:** Takes way more time, you might get biased

### **Contextual Inquiry (CI)** 🔍
- You observe them (like non-participant)
- BUT you also ask questions WHILE they work (like participant)
- It's **"Observation + Interview"** at the same time
- Think of it as a **master-apprentice relationship** - they're the expert teaching YOU

## Understanding "Objective"

**Instructor Explanation:**

### ❌ **NOT Objective (Biased):**
You think: *"Ugh, I HATE that app, it's so buggy. Alex is probably frustrated too."*
- You're letting YOUR feelings affect what you see
- You might write down "Alex looked frustrated" when they're actually just concentrating

### ✅ **Objective (Unbiased):**
You observe: *"Alex opened App A, scrolled for 15 seconds, closed it. Opened App B, tapped 3 times, frowned, closed it. Opened App C and placed order."*
- You're recording FACTS, not assumptions
- No guessing about feelings - just describing what happened

**Being objective = Seeing things as they ARE, not as you THINK they are**

## The Memory Problem & Better Approach

**Instructor:**

**If you wait until the END:**

You ask: *"Hey, why did you check that group chat 5 minutes ago?"*

Alex responds: *"Group chat? Oh... uh... I think I was checking if my roommate wanted food? Or was I just seeing if anyone replied? I don't remember."*

**The problem:** People forget details FAST. Their memory gets fuzzy.

### 🎯 **The Better Approach: Option C**

**Contextual Inquiry Rule:** Ask questions **WHILE they work**, but don't disrupt the flow.

**Here's how it looks:**

1. Alex opens the group chat
2. You notice they scroll, read something
3. They pause (natural break!)
4. You ask: *"I noticed you checked the group chat - what are you looking for?"*
5. Alex explains: *"Oh, we share promo codes here. Seeing if anyone posted a new one."*

**Key Principle:**
> **"Knowledge transfer happens when people talk about HOW they work WHILE they are doing the work."**

## Interview Question Types to AVOID

### ❌ **1. Leading Questions**
- *"Why do you enjoy this app SO MUCH?"* (assumes they enjoy it)
- Better: *"What do you think about this app?"*

### ❌ **2. Closed/Binary Questions**  
- *"So you use this app every day?"* (just yes/no)
- Better: *"Tell me about how you use this app"*

### ❌ **3. Compound Questions**
- *"How often do you order and what's your favorite food and do you split with roommates?"*
- Better: Pick ONE thing to ask about at a time

## Probing Techniques

When Alex mentions something interesting, you **probe deeper** with follow-up questions:

### **Good Probing Questions:**

**Silent probe:** Just pause, they'll keep talking  
**Echo probe:** *"Complicated?"* (repeat their word)  
**Tell me more:** *"Can you walk me through what happens?"*  
**Specific instance:** *"Tell me about the LAST time this happened"*

### ⚠️ **The "Usually" Trap**

**❌ Bad question:**  
*"So how do you USUALLY split bills with roommates?"*

**Why bad?** They'll give you a generic, idealized answer.

**✅ Better question:**  
*"Tell me about the LAST TIME you tried to split an order. What happened?"*

## Lesson 3: Focus Groups

**Instructor:** Instead of interviewing people one-by-one, you get **6-9 students in a room together** to discuss.

### **What is a Focus Group?**

**Definition:** A facilitator runs a 1-2 hour workshop/meeting with a small group

**Key difference:**
- **Interview:** 1-on-1, deep dive into ONE person's experience
- **Focus Group:** Group discussion, people BUILD ON each other's ideas

### 💡 **The Magic of Focus Groups:**

**Scenario:** You ask: *"What's wrong with the library's group study rooms?"*

**What happens:**
- **Student A:** "They're too small"
- **Student B:** "Yeah! And there's no whiteboard"
- **Student C:** "Wait, Room 304 has a whiteboard, but it's always booked"
- **Student A:** "OH! That's the problem - we can't reserve rooms in advance!"

**They sparked each other's thoughts!**

### ⚠️ **The Dark Side:**

**Problem:** Group dynamics can SILENCE people.

- The loud student dominates
- Shy students don't speak up
- Someone shares unpopular opinion, gets judged, everyone stays quiet

## Method Selection Guide

| **Method** | **When to Use** | **Strength** | **Weakness** |
|------------|-----------------|--------------|--------------|
| **Observation** | Understand actual behavior | See reality, not what they SAY | Don't know WHY |
| **Contextual Inquiry** | Understand workflow in context | Rich data + explanations | Time intensive |
| **Interview (1-on-1)** | Deep dive into individual experience | Detailed personal insights | Slow, can't scale |
| **Focus Group** | Generate ideas, understand shared perspectives | Fast, people build on ideas | Group dynamics issues |
| **Survey** | Validate with large numbers | Scalable, quantitative data | Shallow, no "why" |

## Survey vs Interview Comparison

| **Aspect** | **Interview** | **Survey** |
|------------|---------------|------------|
| **Sample Size** | 5-15 people | 100-1000+ people |
| **Data Type** | Qualitative (stories, WHY) | Quantitative (numbers, WHAT) |
| **Questions** | Flexible, can probe | Fixed, same for everyone |
| **Time** | 30-60 min per person | 3-5 minutes total |
| **Cost** | High effort | Lower effort (scales) |
| **When** | Early (explore) | Late (validate) |

## **The 4 Principles of CI - Detailed**

### **1️⃣ CONTEXT**
**Rule:** Go to WHERE the work actually happens.

**Why?** Environment affects behavior.

**Example:**
- ❌ BAD: Ask chef how they cook in conference room
- ✅ GOOD: Watch chef in their actual kitchen

> "Useful for understanding underlying work structures rather than people's perceptions"

### **2️⃣ PARTNERSHIP**
**Rule:** Master/Apprentice - THEY are expert, YOU learn.

**Why?** Knowledge transfers when people explain HOW they work WHILE doing it.

**You're not interrogating - you're learning their craft.**

### **3️⃣ INTERPRETATION**
**Rule:** Observe → Form interpretation → CHECK with them.

**Example:**
- See: Doctor clicks "Save" 3 times rapidly
- Your interpretation: "Seems like you're not sure if it saved?"
- Doctor confirms: "Yeah! System doesn't give feedback"

> "Double-check interpretations with participants"

### **4️⃣ FOCUS**
**Rule:** Start narrow, stay open to surprises.

**Example:**
- Focus: "Understand medication tracking system"
- Surprise: Nurses keep PAPER notebook alongside digital
- Ask: "Why the notebook?"
- Insight: "System crashes, so we track manually as backup"

> "Surprises or odd actions are opportunities to ask questions"

## **What are "Question Forks"?**

A **fork** = answers branch into different paths (choose-your-own-adventure)

**Example:**

**Q1:** "Do you use Snapchat?"
- If YES → Q2a: "How often?"
- If NO → Q5: "Why not?"

**This is a FORK** - different paths through survey.

## **Why use flowchart?**

Complex forks need visualization:
```
Q1: Use Snapchat? → YES → Q2: How often? → Daily → Q3: Features?
                   ↓ NO
                   Q5: Why not? → Privacy → Q6: Alternatives?
```

## **The Length Problem:**

**Issue:** Forks make surveys accidentally LONG for some.

- Person A (all NO) → 2 minutes (10 questions)
- Person B (all YES) → 15 minutes (40 questions)

Person B feels frustrated!

### **📄 Page 47: Question Content/Wording**

## **1️⃣ Self-Check:**

**"Do I need to know that?"**
- Don't ask unnecessary questions
- Every question = more dropout risk

**"Do I need to know it at that level of detail?"**
- Don't ask for MORE precision than needed

**Example:**
- ❌ "Exact monthly spending down to cent?"
- ✅ "Approximately: <$50, $50-100, $100-150, >$150?"

## **2️⃣ Word Choice Impacts Results:**

**Example:**
- "How often do you USE Snapchat?"
- vs "How frequently do you CHECK Snapchat?"

Seem similar but might get different answers!

## **3️⃣ Pilot Testing:**

**Process:**
1. Test with friends (honest, find confusing questions)
2. Test with non-friends (represent real audience)

Friends might understand weird wording. Strangers reveal REAL problems.

## **4️⃣ Question Types:**

### **Open-Ended:**
- No predetermined answers
- User types freely
- **Example:** "What do you think about library?"
- **Pro:** Rich responses | **Con:** Hard to analyze

### **Close-Ended (Scalar):**

**A) Nominal (Categories - no order):**
- Just labels, no ranking
- **Example:** "Major? Engineering / Business / Arts / Science"

**B) Ordinal (Ordered Categories):**
- Categories with clear order
- **Example:** "Satisfied? Very Unsatisfied / Unsatisfied / Neutral / Satisfied / Very Satisfied"

### **Partially Closed:**
- Predetermined options + "Other: ______"
- **Example:** "Apps? □ UberEats □ DoorDash □ Other: _____"

### **📄 Page 48: Survey Tips**

## **1️⃣ Question applies & requires answer:**
- ❌ BAD: "How often use 3D printer?" when most don't know it exists
- ✅ GOOD: First ask awareness, then usage

## **2️⃣ ONE question at a time:**
- ❌ BAD: "How often visit and what do you do?"
- ✅ GOOD: Separate into Q1 and Q2

## **3️⃣ Simple, familiar words; no acronyms:**
- ❌ BAD: "How often use OPAC system?"
- ✅ GOOD: "How often use library's online catalog?"

## **4️⃣ Complete but simple sentences:**
- ❌ COMPLEX: "In consideration of your utilization patterns..."
- ✅ SIMPLE: "When you use the library..."

## **5️⃣ Avoid double negatives:**
- ❌ BAD: "Do you disagree library is not helpful?"
- ✅ GOOD: "Do you find library helpful?"

## **6️⃣ Match question to responses:**
- ❌ MISMATCH: "How satisfied?" → Yes/No
- ✅ MATCH: "How satisfied?" → Scale

## **7️⃣ Avoid biases from formatting:**
- Don't bold/color certain options
- Visual emphasis = bias

## **8️⃣ Check on various screens:**
- Test on desktop, phone, tablet
- Ensure readability everywhere

## **Collaborative & Participatory Methods**

Methods where **users actively participate in designing the solution** (not just answering).

## **1️⃣ Participatory Design:**

**Concept:** Users become CO-DESIGNERS, not just research subjects.

**How:**
- Bring users into design sessions
- They sketch, build prototypes, make decisions
- You facilitate, THEY create

**Example:**
- Give them paper, markers, sticky notes
- "Design your ideal library app"
- Learn what matters by watching them design

**Key:** Users CREATE, not just TALK.

## **2️⃣ JAD/JDP:**

**Concept:** Structured workshop where stakeholders (users, developers, designers, managers) meet to make decisions FAST.

**How:**
- 2-5 day intensive workshop
- All key people in one room
- Facilitator guides
- Make decisions about requirements, features, design
- Everyone agrees in real-time

**Example:**
- Day 1: Define requirements together
- Day 2: Map workflows
- Day 3: Prioritize features

**Key benefit:** FAST consensus - instead of 3 months of meetings, get alignment in days.

## **Why "Collaborative"?**

Multiple people work TOGETHER at same time, rather than researcher interviewing separately.
