## Page 6: Observation Definition

- **Definition:** A team member observes stakeholders' behavior while using a similar or competitor's product.
    
- **Usefulness:** Identifying pain points and areas for product improvement.
    

## Page 7: Participant Observation

- **Definition:** Researcher enters the community and actively takes part in the observed activity.
    
- **Use:** Understanding a phenomenon by experiencing it firsthand.
    

## Page 8: Participant Observation Pros & Cons

- **Pros:**
    
    - Researcher gets hands-on experience; gains insight into meanings, viewpoints, values, and problems.
        
    - Develops insights where direct questioning is ineffective.
        
- **Cons:**
    
    - Risk of bias due to empathy towards the observer.
        
    - May raise ethical concerns.
        
    - Time-consuming.
        

## Page 9: Non-Participant Observation

- **Definition:** Researcher observes by entering the community but remains completely separate from the observed activity.
    
- **Use:** Understanding a phenomenon by viewing what is happening with their own eyes.
    

## Page 10: Non-Participant Observation Pros & Cons

- **Pros:**
    
    - Allows researchers to be more open-minded (not sympathetically involved).
        
    - Allows researchers to make judgments and observe body language.
        
- **Cons:**
    
    - Risk of inaccurate data from observing from a distance.
        
    - Time-consuming.
        
    - People may change their behavior if aware of being observed.
        

## Page 11: Hawthorne Effect (Observer Effect)

- **Definition:** Individuals modify their behavior in response to their awareness of being observed.
    
- **Origin:** Named for experiments at the Hawthorne Works factories in the 1920s & 1930s.
    
- **Avoidance:** Build rapport, normalize the observation, use multiple methods.
    

## Page 13: Interview Formats

- **Unstructured Interview:**
    
    - Little knowledge about the topic.
        
    - No guide; exploratory, high flexibility ("off the cuff").
        
    - Interviewer is the apprentice/learner.
        
- **Semi-Structured Interview:**
    
    - Some knowledge about the topic.
        
    - Less strictly defined guide with themes/questions.
        
    - Deviations are allowed and encouraged (probing).
        
- **Structured Interview:**
    
    - Lots of knowledge about the topic.
        
    - Strictly defined list of questions.
        
    - No deviations allowed across interviews or participants.
        

## Page 15: Interview Tips (Comfort and Empathy)

- Aim for participants to be comfortable.
    
- Let participants finish their thoughts; make them feel heard (take notes, eye contact, acknowledgments like "I see").
    
- Show empathy (e.g., "That must have been frustrating").
    

## Page 16: Interview Tips (Preparation)

- Have questions prepared (a guide) but allow going off-script.
    
- Anticipate different responses based on participant experience.
    
- Focus on soliciting information efficiently (one focused question vs. multiple exploratory ones).
    

## Page 17: Interview Tips (Question Wording)

- **Avoid leading questions:**
    
    - _Bad:_ "Why do you enjoy using the Acme product so much?"
        
    - _Better:_ "Why do you use the Acme product?" or "What do you think about X?"
        
- **Avoid closed questions:**
    
    - _Bad:_ "So, you use the Acme product every morning?"
        
    - _Better:_ "Can you tell me about how you use Acme?"
        
- Practice follow-up questions.
    
- Use prompts to encourage detail: Silent, echo, agreeing, "tell me more," clarifying.
    

## Page 18: Interview Stages | Preparation

1. **Research:** Come in with lots of knowledge about the person and the topic.
    
2. **Prepare:** Draft an interview protocol (set of themes) for natural conversation flow.
    
3. **Be Participant-Centered:** Ask what day, time, and location works for the individual.
    
4. **Be Early, Be Prepared:** Arrive 10 minutes early.
    
5. **Define Roles:** Ideally two team members (one leads the interview, one takes notes/photos). Ask for permission to audio record.
    

## Page 19: Interview Stages | Execution

1. **Introduce:** Briefly describe research and goals (a good interview is a friendly conversation).
    
2. **Dig:** Ask open-ended questions for stories, feelings, and emotions. Ask "why." Ask about a specific instance, not "usually."
    
3. **Notice Inconsistencies:** Pay attention when what people say differs from what they do; do not correct them.
    
4. **Pay Attention to Nonverbal Cues:** Take notes on what they say and don't say; be aware of body language and emotions.
    

## Page 20: Interview Stages | Execution Common Mistakes

- **Avoid compound questions:** (e.g., "How often do you check Snapchat and what is the reason?")
    
- **Avoid leading/directive/binary questions:** (These imply a right answer.)
    
- **Don't fall into the trap of leading expressions:** (e.g., nodding, "aha," "that's great" – balance with not being deadpan).
    
- **Don't suggest answers:** Even if they pause.
    
- **Don't be afraid of silence:** Allows reflection for deeper reveals.
    

## Page 23: Focus Groups: Benefits

- Participants can build on each other's responses.
    
- Can help clarify users' mental models and vocabulary/language.
    
- Time-efficient method for the researcher.
    
- Can yield rich qualitative insights.
    

## Page 24: Focus Groups Definition

- **Definition:** A qualitative research method where a facilitator conducts a 1-2 hour meeting/workshop with a group of 6-9 people.
    
- **Focus:** Maintain group focus on certain topics (issues/concerns about experiences with a product or service).
    

## Page 25: Focus Groups: Risks

- Group dynamics may impact how much (or how little) people share.
    

## Page 26: Focus Groups: Effective Execution

- Recruit participants representative of target audiences.
    
- Treat as a workshop: Make participants comfortable with verbal or nonverbal participation.
    
    - Use an easy warmup activity.
        
    - Include both written and verbal participation opportunities.
        
- Have a written plan and guide.
    

## Page 29: Contextual Inquiry (CI) Definition

- **Concept:** Applied design ethnography.
    
- **Core Premise:**
    
    - Go where the customer works.
        
    - Observe the customer as he or she works.
        
    - Talk to the customer about the work.
        
- **Method:** Effectively **observation + interview**.
    

## Page 30: Contextual Inquiry (CI): Four Principles

1. Context
    
2. Partnership
    
3. Interpretation
    
4. Focus
    

## Page 31: Four Principles of CI: Context

- Must be "where the work happens."
    
- Useful for understanding underlying work structures rather than people's perceptions.
    

## Page 32: Four Principles of CI: Partnership

- Aim for a **master/apprenticeship model** (Researcher is the apprentice).
    
- Knowledge transfer happens when people talk about _how_ they work _while_ they are doing the work.
    

## Page 33: Four Principles of CI: Interpretation

- All data must be interpreted for meaning before developing design implications.
    
- Merge observation insights with question insights.
    
- Double-check interpretations with participants (they can make changes).
    

## Page 34: Four Principles of CI: Focus

- Enter the field with a narrower focus, but be open to expanding assumptions.
    
- Surprises or odd actions are opportunities to ask questions.
    
- Aim to see the participant's perspective.
    

## Page 37: Contextual Inquiry: Guidelines (Access and Rapport)

- Must gain access to the site and informants.
    
- Must gain confidence and trust.
    
- Recognize potential reactions: excitement, culture shock, emotional impact of entering/leaving.
    

## Page 38: Contextual Inquiry: Guidelines (Recording)

- Methods: Video recording, note taking and memos, interviewing.
    
- Be mindful of access permissions and participant comfort levels.
    

## Page 39: Contextual Inquiry: Guidelines (What to Record)

- Begin with research questions.
    
- Do not overly constrain yourself.
    
- Take notes on: what is directly relevant, what strikes you as interesting, and everything else.
    

## Page 40: Contextual Inquiry: Guidelines (Process of Recording)

- Data quality depends on the researcher.
    
- Record in the moment if possible, or immediately after the event.
    
- Write down important things first, then replay the day and slot them into order.
    

## Page 41: Contextual Inquiry Strengths

- Extremely rich data (watch what people do and talk to them).
    
- "Real world" and unfiltered.
    
- Methods are led by participant needs/interests, not preconceived notions.
    

## Page 42: Contextual Inquiry Weaknesses

- Participants may not know what is important to the researcher (probing helps but isn't sufficient).
    
- People's memories are flawed (say one thing in an interview, do another in practice).
    
- Resource-intensive (making generalization challenging).
    
- Participants may find it awkward to be watched.
    

## Page 43: Contextual Inquiry Summary Sketch

- **Goal:** In-depth understanding of context, activities, tasks, pain points, behavior, process, roles, inputs, outputs.
    
- **Key Action:** Be a "fly on the wall," observe everything, conduct short interviews.
    
- **Relationship:** "Master-Apprentice" (ask for demonstration instead of explanation).
    
- **After CI:** Analyze data (e.g., Affinity Diagramming) to find patterns.
    
- **Value:** Validating assumptions, finding clues/signals (e.g., sticky-note cheat sheets), discovering unexpected artifacts/actors, creating realistic personas/scenarios/journey maps.
    

## Page 44: Surveys and Questionnaires

- **Definition:** Structured set of questions a person responds to.
    
- **Characteristics:** More structured than qualitative techniques.
    

## Page 45: Getting People to Respond (Survey Tips)

- **Increase perceived rewards:** Provide info about goals, ask for help, say thank you, give tangible rewards, make it interesting.
    
- **Establish trust:** Lean on your role/organization, give payment in advance, ensure confidentiality/security/anonymity.
    
- **Decrease perceived costs:** Make it convenient, as short as possible, minimize sensitive information requests.
    

## Page 46: Surveys and Questionnaires (Question Flow)

- Answers can lead to question forks (e.g., "If you have used Snapchat in the last week...").
    
- Use a flowchart to manage forks.
    
- Be aware of length issues resulting from forks.
    

## Page 47: Surveys and Questionnaires (Question Content/Wording)

- **Self-Check:** Ask: "Do I need to know that?" and "Do I need to know it at that level of detail?"
    
- Word choice impacts results. Pilot with friends, then non-friends.
    
- **Question Types:**
    
    - Open-ended.
        
    - Close-ended (scalar): Nominal (categories), Ordinal (ordered categories).
        
    - Partially closed: Has an "other" option.
        

## Page 48: Surveys and Questionnaires (Tips)

- Make sure the question applies to the respondent and requires an answer.
    
- Ask one question at a time.
    
- Use simple, familiar, well-defined words; no acronyms.
    
- Use complete but simple sentences.
    
- Avoid double negatives.
    
- Match the question to the responses.
    
- Avoid accidental biases from color, bolding, fonts, etc.
    
- Check spacing and alignment on various screens/orientations.
